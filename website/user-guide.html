User guide

Your first hour

You've just launched the game and you're looking at a valley. This guide takes you from there to your first working overpass, and then to the habit of mind that makes the rest of the corridor click.

Step one

Getting oriented

You begin in the Central Canadian Rockies — Banff, Jasper, and the Bow Valley. It's the only region open at the start, and it's the right place to learn: the real wildlife overpasses along the Trans-Canada Highway here are the most-studied in the world, and they work.

The heads-up display keeps four things in the corners and nothing over the middle of the map:

What's on screen
Budget Top left. Your balance, plus the last donation you received. You start with 50,000.
Season calendar Top right. Current season, year, and day, with a four-segment ring showing how far through the year you are.
Build toolbar Bottom left. Add crossing, Inspect, and Info.
Milestone track Bottom right. A compact "Corridor n/12" counter. Click it for the full list.

Controls worth knowing now

Pause is free

Everything works while paused — building, buying information, opening panels. The game never punishes you for stopping to think, and opening a panel won't secretly unpause you.

Step two

Reading the land

You don't place habitat in Wildlife Crossing. The habitat is already there — forest, alpine meadow, grassland, wetland, cliffs — and it's been cut into pieces by roads, fences, and towns. Your job is to read those pieces and work out which ones are worth reconnecting.

A contiguous run of compatible terrain is a patch. Click one with the Inspect tool and you'll get a habitat-quality band. Quality comes from four things: how suitable the terrain is, how big the patch is, how well it connects to other patches, and how much of its perimeter is pressed up against a road or an urban zone.

  • Poor
  • Fair
  • Good
  • Excellent

The terrain map is never hidden — it's real geography, and hiding it would defeat the point. What's hidden is the living layer. Until you buy information, quality shows only as those coarse bands, populations read "unknown," and the places animals die trying to cross are invisible.

Buying information

The Info tool sells four products. Each purchase is permanent, and each one is worth it only if it would change what you'd build.

Habitat assessment1,000 / areaNumeric habitat quality and patch sizes

Worth buying first

In a region you've never worked in, the population survey usually earns its 2,000 back fastest. An endangered species you didn't know was there changes your whole plan; a habitat number you can already guess from the colour band doesn't.

Step three

What the animals need

Eight species share the corridor, and they don't want the same things. Each has a habitat set, a range, a preferred kind of crossing, and a seasonal rhythm. Three of those matter for almost every decision you make.

Range and viable size

Every species needs a minimum amount of connected, suitable habitat before it can hold a population. Below that threshold, numbers drift downward — slowly, without drama, but they drift. A grizzly needs an enormous network; a lynx needs a moderate one. This is why a crossing that merges two medium patches can flip a species from declining to recovering in one move.

Crossing preference

Animals are likelier to use their preferred kind of crossing, but they're never strictly forbidden from the others — with one exception. Pronghorn will not go under things. They evolved on open ground where seeing a predator coming is everything, and no amount of budget will talk them into a tunnel.

In version 1 the overpass is the only crossing you can build. That's deliberate: species that prefer underpasses (wolves, lynx) or broad corridors (caribou, wolverines) are harder to serve well right now, and will get easier when those crossing types ship.

Seasonal pattern

Some species migrate, some hibernate, some are present all year. Grizzlies leave the simulation entirely each winter. Elk and pronghorn move on a schedule and push hard against barriers when they do.

Mortality is the quiet cost

An animal stepping onto a hazardous tile — a road, an unfenced highway — rolls against that terrain's mortality rate, and can die there. Crossing on infrastructure you built carries no risk at all. Every crossing you finish is a route where that roll stops happening.

Step four

Choosing a segment and building

Click Add crossing in the toolbar. This puts you in selection mode.

  1. Zoom in. Keep scrolling until the hexes are large enough for segment detail to switch on. You'll know it happened because the connectivity overlay appears — a soft orange-to-teal wash where orange is badly fragmented and teal is well connected. The three worst segments in view pulse gently.
  2. Hover a road. Only impassable or hazardous segments can be selected. Hovering plain terrain does nothing; hovering a valid segment highlights it.
  3. Click it. A confirmation panel opens with the segment's name, your current budget, and a one-line note about what it would do for connectivity. Clicking outside the panel, or pressing Esc, cancels without spending anything.
  4. Build. Confirming takes you to construction. An overpass costs 5,000 per tile of road it spans, so a three-tile highway crossing costs 15,000.
A partial span is worth nothing

The crossing has to cover every dangerous tile in the segment. Cover four tiles of a five-tile highway and you have not built a route — you've built an expensive shelf. Check the span before you confirm.

Knowing whether it worked

Within a few in-game days, animals will start pathfinding through the new route — you'll see the crossing cue fire and a "+n crossed safely" count flash on screen. Over the following weeks the patches on either side recompute upward, populations stop declining, and the first donations tied to that crossing land in your budget.

Donations arrive monthly. You always get a base grant of 1,000, plus income for each crossing based on how many animals used it, how threatened those species are (common ×1, vulnerable ×2, endangered ×3), and how fragmented the link was before you fixed it. Recovery milestones pay one-time bonuses on top.

That formula is the whole design argument in one line: building thoughtfully pays measurably better than building randomly.

You cannot get stuck

If your budget ever falls below the cheapest thing you could do, a small community fundraising trickle of 500 a month kicks in until you're moving again. Running low is a squeeze, not a dead end.

Step five

Seasons and the passage of time

One season is fifteen minutes at 1× speed, so an in-game day passes in about ten seconds and a full year takes an hour. The season ring in the top right is there so you can see what's coming.

Seasons change three things:

  • Who's present. Migratory species appear in some patches only at certain times of year; hibernating species vanish entirely.
  • How badly they want to move. During a migration season, animals push much harder against barriers — and use new crossings far more.
  • What the terrain does. Rivers freeze, wetlands flood. Routes open and close.

The practical consequence is simple: finish crossings before migration seasons, not during them. The same overpass on the same segment can earn dramatically different donations depending on which side of the season change it opens.

Step six

Tips and advanced play

Chase the pinch points, not the pretty places

The most valuable crossing is rarely in the most beautiful patch. It's at the narrowest, most degraded gap between two patches that are each nearly viable on their own. The connectivity overlay is telling you exactly this — the orange is where the money is.

Merge, don't decorate

A crossing that links two patches into one network is worth more than two crossings that each improve a patch already doing fine. Ask "what does this join?" before "what does this improve?"

Read the entity before you court it

The twelve regions are stewarded by nine different governing bodies, and they don't measure you the same way. Transport authorities count completed infrastructure. Federal land agencies care about crossing usage and reduced road mortality. Territorial governments want sustained stewardship over time. First Nations partnerships weigh demonstrated ecological outcomes and the respect you've shown. A 1,500 liaison briefing tells you which is which.

Progress shows as four stages — Introduced → Engaged → Trusted → Partnered — with the entity's top three priorities listed and tracked. Reaching the threshold unlocks their region and pays a one-time bonus. Trust is never revoked; neglecting an entity only slows how fast new trust accrues elsewhere.

The Crown of the Continent is designed to be your first unlock — a deliberately low threshold that introduces the partnership mechanic early.

Play the long game

Per-region milestones mark your first crossing, all priority segments addressed, and all resident species stable. The capstone — Continental Connection — is an unbroken chain of connected habitat from Greater Yellowstone to the Greater Mackenzie Mountains. It plays a celebration and then the game carries on. Nothing is locked behind it.
