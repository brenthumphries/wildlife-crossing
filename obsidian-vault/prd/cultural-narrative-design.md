## Purpose of this document

Wildlife Crossing involves land within the Y2Y corridor that is home to, and
in many cases under the jurisdiction of, First Nations and Indigenous communities.
The game's progression system requires the player to earn consent from these
communities before building crossing infrastructure on their lands. This document
defines how those communities, and other cultural and governmental entities in
the game, are portrayed — the narrative framing, the representation principles,
the consultation process, and the standards that apply to any content involving
real peoples and places.

The mechanical system for earning governmental permission is defined separately
in [[governmental-permissions]]. This document covers the layer of meaning on
top of that system.

---

## Design intent

The game should reflect, accurately and respectfully, that:

- Indigenous communities have deep ecological knowledge and land stewardship
  practices that often produce better outcomes than conventional conservation
  approaches.
- Consent from First Nations is not a game mechanic to optimise around — it is
  a representation of a real relationship that requires demonstrated respect,
  not just demonstrated performance.
- The Y2Y corridor passes through the traditional territories of many First
  Nations whose connection to this land long predates the national and
  provincial borders the game also depicts.

The game is an educational tool as well as entertainment. Players should come
away with a truer understanding of who stewards this landscape and why their
involvement in conservation decisions is not optional or incidental.

---

## Principles

1. **Named communities require consent and accuracy.** If the game names a
   specific First Nation or Indigenous community, that portrayal must be
   accurate and, where feasible, developed in consultation with that community.
   Generic or composite stand-ins are preferable to inaccurate specifics.

2. **Indigenous stewardship is centred, not backgrounded.** The game should not
   portray First Nations as gatekeepers the player must appease to access land.
   Their role is as stewards with legitimate authority and ecological expertise —
   the player is seeking partnership, not permission in a bureaucratic sense.

3. **The game does not speak for Indigenous peoples.** Dialogue, worldview, and
   cultural specifics should be developed with input from people who hold that
   knowledge, not invented by the development team.

4. **Failure to earn consent should feel like a natural consequence, not a
   punishment.** If a player has not demonstrated sufficient stewardship, a
   community declining to permit construction should feel understandable and
   motivating, consistent with the "cozy, not stressful" design pillar.

5. **Government agencies are also characterised thoughtfully.** Federal and
   provincial/state agencies have real institutional cultures, histories, and
   priorities that should inform their portrayal — not reduced to
   bureaucratic obstacles.

## Open questions

- ~~**Should external cultural consultants be engaged?**~~
  *Resolved: yes — required. Formal engagement of Indigenous cultural advisors is
  a hard gate before any First Nations narrative content ships. The development
  pipeline includes a cultural-review step for: the three fictional communities'
  design, sub-area naming, dialogue, and the joint-stewardship event content.
  Budgeted as a project line item.*

- ~~**How are specific First Nations communities identified and portrayed?**~~
  *Resolved: fictional, region-specific communities. The three First Nations
  partnerships are respectfully crafted fictional communities grounded in real
  regional context (language family, ecological practices, geography), explicitly
  framed in-game as fictional. Real Nations' territories are acknowledged in
  sub-area info panels and a land acknowledgment in the game's opening. This
  avoids misrepresenting real Nations without their consent while keeping the
  educational content specific rather than generic.*

- ~~**What is the narrative framing of the player's role relative to Indigenous
  communities?**~~
  *Resolved: the player is an outsider — a coordinator for a fictional
  conservation non-profit — seeking partnership, not permission in a bureaucratic
  sense. The org has an existing reputation that the player's actions build on;
  First Nations interactions are framed as relationship-building between
  organizations and communities.*

- ~~**How are First Nations ecological knowledge and practices surfaced in
  gameplay?**~~
  *Resolved: Indigenous knowledge appears in mechanics, not just text. Partnership
  with a First Nations entity confers gameplay effects reflecting stewardship
  expertise: movement-corridor data in their territory is revealed free of charge;
  co-stewarded habitat patches receive a baseline habitat-quality bonus; and
  "guided placement" hints highlight high-impact segments in their sub-area.
  Indigenous knowledge is mechanically better information and better outcomes —
  matching the real-world record of Indigenous-led conservation.*
